# Sim Intelliegence World — Game Specification

**Short name:** SI World  
**Status:** Early design / pre-production  
**Last updated:** 2026-08-10  
**Working directory:** `~/Documents/Vibecode/Sim Intelliegence World`  
**Target platforms:** macOS desktop and Windows PC desktop  
**Distribution:** Paid commercial game on Steam

## 1. Product Summary

**Sim Intelliegence World (SI World)** is a desktop social simulation game built around named locally powered AI NPCs and a larger supporting cast of deterministic ambient residents. The player controls an introverted male protagonist who walks around the world, meets people, develops relationships, and hopes to find a wife.

Named-NPC dialogue and high-level conversational behavior are generated by a bundled local model. **Qwen3.5-9B** at `Q4_K_M` is the target, subject to the locked performance gate and smaller-model fallback. The core AI experience works offline and does not require players to install Ollama, Python, Docker, or a separate model-running application.

The central design goal is for NPCs to feel like individual people with their own personalities, biographies, knowledge, memories, boundaries, and evolving opinions of the protagonist.

## 2. Locked Product Decisions

The following decisions are currently locked:

- The game is called **Sim Intelliegence World**, abbreviated **SI World**.
- SI World is a desktop game for **macOS and Windows PC**.
- SI World will be sold commercially on **Steam**.
- SI World is strictly single-player.
- The protagonist is an introverted man who can walk around the world and wants to find a wife.
- The player enters the protagonist's name; authored content and saves use a stable internal protagonist ID rather than assuming one fixed name.
- The game has a darkly funny tone.
- The setting is an exclusive town on a remote island with unusual local rules.
- The protagonist wins a prize that gives him one year of residence on the island and a weekly allowance.
- The prize includes a small private villa in the northwest residential and relaxation neighborhood. It contains a bedroom, bathroom, kitchen, storage, and a social area where invited characters can visit.
- The weekly allowance covers ordinary food and basic daily costs, so the protagonist does not need a normal job to maintain a basic life on the island.
- One weekly allowance covers seven days of basic costs plus a small buffer. Ordinary quests may pay up to one weekly allowance; dangerous quests may pay one to three weekly allowances.
- Quest rewards provide discretionary money for gifts, entertainment, bribes, equipment, luxuries, and other optional advantages.
- The one-year term is backstory and does not create a normal gameplay deadline; the game is not expected to require content through day 365.
- If a save reaches the end of the term, the island reviews his relationships, reputation, crimes, work, and contribution to decide whether he may remain. Marriage may help but is not required.
- The prize is not random: island organizers secretly select the protagonist because he is already a viral meme with a large online following.
- The organizers believe his foolish public reaction, carelessness, and lack of attention make him easy to manage and useful advertising for the island.
- The protagonist's actual judgment and competence are defined by player choices; the viral meme creates an initial public assumption that he is foolish.
- The island's advantages are genuine. There is no larger central conspiracy behind the prize.
- The organizers' deception is limited to hiding that they selected him for natural, entertaining publicity rather than choosing him randomly.
- The protagonist's online fame is a background reputation system. Followers may recognize him, send messages, and react to major events, but the game does not include a separate posting or influencer-management system.
- The first hour is adventure-led: quests, factions, the concealed underworld, and early danger introduce the island before routine life management becomes important.
- The first prototype quest begins when Linda asks the protagonist to check on her boyfriend at Linda's villa. The quest reveals that her boyfriend is abusive and introduces consequential choices, relationship changes, and a lead toward the island's concealed underworld.
- The underworld is discovered gradually through ordinary conversations, harmless-looking errands, and everyday locations that reveal criminal connections.
- After the opening hour, the primary repeating loop is: talk to people, discover leads, complete risky quests, and change relationships and faction standing. Quest outcomes create new conversations, opportunities, and leads that repeat the cycle.
- A quest journal records validated leads, clues, known deadlines, and known locations. It shows a map marker only after the protagonist learns the exact location through conversation, observation, or another validated event.
- Time, money, schedules, reputation, and other life-management systems support and constrain the social-adventure loop. They are not separate equal-priority game loops.
- The protagonist has only two personal-condition needs: energy and health. Food and sleep support those values without creating constant survival pressure.
- The game does not use separate hunger, hygiene, bladder, fun, or social-need meters.
- Ordinary exploration and simulation run in real time with pause, `1×`, and `2×` speed controls.
- At `1×`, one real second equals one game minute, so a full twenty-four-hour game day lasts `24` real minutes. At `2×`, it lasts `12` real minutes.
- The villa bed offers a two-hour nap at any time and overnight sleep until `8:00 AM` when used after `8:00 PM`.
- Napping and overnight sleep advance the authoritative clock and off-screen schedules. Overnight sleep skips the remaining night.
- Starting a conversation automatically pauses world time. World time remains paused until the conversation ends, so local-model generation speed never consumes the player's in-game time.
- The protagonist is legally free to leave the island, but version one does not provide a usable ferry or playable leaving action. The ferry exists as a visible world boundary rather than a functional destination.
- Compared with the outside world, the island has cheaper food, more attractive people, more entertainment, more relaxed policing, and less crime.
- Beneath its safe public life, the island has a substantial hidden underworld involving organized crime, adult prostitution, drugs, and other socially frowned-upon vices.
- Island police quietly tolerate private, consensual adult vice and minor personal drug use, but act firmly against coercion, theft, public disorder, and violence.
- Violence uses contextual actions and short abstract animations rather than real-time twitch combat. Deterministic position, equipment, health, and choices resolve outcomes.
- Crimes create evidence, witnesses, and police attention. Police attention progresses through `noticed`, `questioned`, `wanted`, and `arrest-on-sight` states.
- The protagonist does not permanently die. Defeat may cause injury, arrest, lost time, lost money or items, and reputation damage. Fictional NPCs may die.
- Characters use a simple, cute, stylized 2D design with small animated legs rather than realistic human art.
- The software stack follows the existing Life Sim project: Expo, React Native, strict TypeScript, React Native Web, React Native Skia, Reanimated, Zustand, Zod, React Native StyleSheet, and Expo Audio.
- Only the Life Sim software stack is reused. Its story, autonomous queue mechanics, home layout, pacing, and binding art rules are not SI World requirements.
- The world uses a top-down orthographic 2D tile map with free camera panning and zooming, in the broad interaction category of RimWorld and Prison Architect.
- The version-one world contains four `64×48`-tile neighborhoods arranged as a connected `2×2` square: northwest, northeast, southwest, and southeast.
- The northwest neighborhood is a quiet residential and relaxation district with villas, spas, massage businesses, and a beach.
- The protagonist starts the game in the northwest residential and relaxation neighborhood.
- The first prototype fully authors the northwest residential and relaxation neighborhood. The other three neighborhoods use functional placeholder maps during the prototype.
- The northeast neighborhood is the downtown entertainment and nightlife district with bars, clubs, and visible or concealed vice.
- The southwest neighborhood is the commercial district with restaurants, shopping malls, independent shops, and other retail businesses.
- The southeast neighborhood combines the docks with the police station and the island's governing body.
- Crossing a neighborhood's connected outer edge triggers a short fade and loads the adjacent neighborhood, placing the protagonist at the matching opposite edge.
- Only the current neighborhood is rendered and fully simulated frame by frame. Inactive neighborhoods advance residents through lightweight deterministic schedule and event updates.
- The protagonist uses mouse click-to-move controls. The player clicks a reachable tile or destination and deterministic pathfinding moves him there.
- The camera uses middle-mouse drag, optional edge panning, mouse-wheel `1×`/`2×`/`3×` zoom selection, and `F` to center on the protagonist.
- The map uses `32×32` logical-pixel tiles, and ordinary world characters use `24×30` logical-pixel sprite cells.
- Walkable building interiors exist on the same neighborhood map as their exteriors. Entering a building hides or fades its roof so the interior becomes visible; leaving restores the roof.
- Doors, interior floors, walls, furniture, characters, and pathfinding remain part of the normal neighborhood scene. Ordinary buildings do not load separate interior maps.
- Character art follows Hero Football Manager's heroic-chibi pixel style and reusable identity-generation approach, adapted with SI World clothing and world-movement poses.
- Ordinary walking uses a two-facing billboard system: rear-facing art when moving upward, and front-facing art when moving downward or sideways.
- Each ordinary character has eight final walking atlas cells: two front, two rear, two left, and two right.
- Artists do not hand-draw all eight cells. They draw two front walking frames and two lateral leg shapes. The asset generator derives the rear frames using HFM's existing method and combines the lateral legs with the front-facing head and torso to generate the left and right frames.
- Character source art is organized into legs, torso/clothing, head/face, hair, accessory, and optional held-item layers. The asset generator combines those layers into flat atlas cells before gameplay; the runtime renders the completed cells rather than rebuilding each character from layers every frame.
- Ordinary two-frame walking uses a frame interval in the `130–160 ms` range.
- Walking may use a one-pixel body lean, a one-pixel contact-shadow shift, and a small vertical bounce. Talking may use a one-pixel head or body bob without requiring another authored frame.
- The prototype uses discrete `1×`, `2×`, and `3×` pixel-art zoom levels and never zooms below native `1×`.
- Full left/right profile sprites are not part of the initial art system. If the front-facing horizontal walk is not readable in the prototype, the first fallback is one mirrored three-quarter head/hair view rather than a complete side-facing body redraw.
- The game may show killings, blood, crime, betrayal, addiction, and adult relationships.
- Local dialogue may discuss relevant fictional-adult relationships, consensual adult prostitution, crime, drugs, addiction, and violence. Explicit sexual detail, sexual violence, sexual content involving minors, and sexual content involving real people receive refusal or an authored fade to black.
- Each important NPC tracks Familiarity, Trust, and Attraction as separate deterministic relationship values.
- Romance requires more than high values: the NPC's authored compatibility, boundaries, current circumstances, and direct consent must all permit it.
- Relationship values or generated dialogue can never override an authored hard boundary or lack of consent.
- Relationship progression uses the stages Stranger, Acquaintance, Friend, Dating, Partner, Engaged, and Married. Each transition requires its deterministic values, authored compatibility and boundaries, current circumstances, and direct consent.
- Rejection records an authored or validated reason. A changeable reason may be reconsidered only after the underlying circumstance changes; an authored hard boundary remains permanent.
- The player may make morally bad choices, but those choices have believable consequences.
- Quest consequences are layered. Social consequences such as lost trust, rejection, betrayal, damaged relationships, reputation changes, and blocked faction access occur more often. Serious choices may also cause money or item loss, police attention, arrest, injury, or death.
- Severe consequences must follow understandable player actions and world rules rather than arriving as arbitrary punishment.
- People and organizations depicted in the game are fictional.
- Version one contains `8–12` named full-AI NPCs and `20–40` ambient residents.
- Full-AI NPCs receive free-text local-model conversations, dedicated authored context, and persistent conversational memory.
- Ambient residents use deterministic schedules, movement, and authored short dialogue. They do not invoke the local model or maintain persistent conversational memory.
- Each NPC has four to six daily schedule blocks. Inactive-neighborhood residents advance directly between deterministic schedule milestones.
- The target local foundation model is **Qwen3.5-9B** at `Q4_K_M`, subject to the locked hardware and performance gate. If it fails that gate, the game must use a smaller bundled model rather than ship unacceptable performance.
- The desktop shell is Electron. Its main process supervises one bundled loopback-only `llama-server` child process.
- The performance gate targets a `16 GB` RAM machine, no more than `3` seconds to first visible text, at least `8` generated tokens per second, and `60 FPS` rendering during generation on named baseline hardware.
- The selected local model remains loaded for the entire gameplay session. It is not unloaded while the player is playing.
- The game is self-contained; players do not need Ollama or another separately installed local-LLM manager.
- Every important NPC has a dedicated directory.
- Each NPC uses three logical context files:
  - `personality.md`
  - `biography.md`
  - `interaction.md`
- The world has its own content/state directory.
- The main character has a dedicated directory.
- Conversations can unlock validated facts, memories, relationship changes, common interests, and other gameplay variables.
- Mutable interaction and world state are saved per save slot on the player's computer, outside the installed Steam game content.
- The player may create a manual save from any stable gameplay state except during an active NPC conversation or a neighborhood transition.
- Three rotating autosaves occur after sleep, after a completed neighborhood transition, and after a major quest outcome is fully applied.
- Versioned JSON is the authoritative production save format. Markdown prompt views are generated from validated state for model context and are not authoritative saves.
- Home invitations are validated dialogue actions. An NPC may accept, reject, or suggest another time; accepted invitations become deterministic scheduled events.
- The setting is fictional and contemporary without stating a real-world calendar year.
- Dialogue is text-first, supported by short authored laughs, sighs, greetings, and other vocal sounds rather than generated full speech.
- Version one does not require Steam Deck support, but its controls and interface must preserve a practical later support path.
- Each save pins its model, prompt, content, and engine versions. Updates require explicit compatible migration and never silently change old NPC behavior.
- The LLM can propose dialogue, intent, emotion, actions, memories, and unlocks, but deterministic game code validates and applies all persistent changes.
- Model proposals use a closed scene-specific JSON schema. Invalid output receives one retry and then safe fallback dialogue with no persistent change.

## 3. Player Fantasy and Core Experience

The intended player experience is a social world in which relationships develop through conversation, shared experiences, discovered compatibility, and remembered behavior.

The presentation uses deliberate contrast: simple, cute 2D characters inhabit a world that can contain adult crime, dangerous relationships, addiction, betrayal, blood, and death. The prevailing tone is darkly funny rather than realistic or relentlessly grim. Mature actions remain consequential within the simulation even when their visual presentation is abstract.

### 3.1 Opening premise

The introverted protagonist wins an unusual prize: permission to live for one year in an exclusive town on a remote island. The prize includes a weekly allowance, so he can begin exploring and socializing without immediately needing a job. In the fiction he is legally free to leave, but version one does not implement a usable ferry or playable departure. Food is cheaper, residents are generally more attractive, entertainment is better, policing is more relaxed, and crime is lower than in the outside world.

The public story is that he won. In reality, island organizers chose him after a foolish public reaction made him a widely followed meme. They see him as careless, inattentive, easy to manage, and useful advertising. The organizers expect his followers to watch island life through him without treating him as a serious observer.

That reputation is not an objective character statistic. The player can behave foolishly and reinforce it, or make careful and intelligent choices while residents continue to underestimate him. Individual NPCs can revise their opinion as they observe his behavior.

The island is not a false paradise, and the protagonist is not secretly imprisoned or enrolled in a larger experiment. Its advertised advantages are real. The organizers' concealed decision to select him for natural publicity is the central deception behind the prize.

The one-year residency is backstory rather than a normal gameplay countdown. It gives the protagonist an immediate reason to enter a socially dense community where he knows nobody, but the game is not designed around reaching day 365. If a save does reach the end of the term, an island residency review considers his relationships, reputation, crimes, work, and contribution. Marriage may help but is not required.

### 3.2 Public safety and the underworld

The island is broadly safe and desirable for ordinary residents. Visible street crime is lower than in the outside world, and residents can enjoy daily life without constant danger. This public reality coexists with a substantial concealed underworld involving organized crime, adult prostitution, drugs, and other vices that respectable island society condemns or refuses to discuss.

The underworld does not make the entire island a false paradise. It gives the player a second social layer to discover. Characters can participate in it, oppose it, profit from it, become harmed by it, or pretend it does not exist.

The player discovers this layer gradually. Ordinary conversations, harmless-looking favors, everyday businesses, and routine errands reveal people and organizations with concealed criminal connections. The game does not begin with an immediate formal invitation into a criminal faction.

Police policy helps both layers coexist. Officers usually ignore private, consensual adult vice and minor personal drug use. They intervene firmly when conduct involves coercion, theft, public disorder, or violence. This produces relaxed policing without making ordinary public life unsafe.

### 3.3 First-hour experience

The opening hour prioritizes island adventure. The first prototype quest begins when Linda asks the protagonist to check on her boyfriend at Linda's villa. The situation reveals that her boyfriend is abusive. The quest introduces journal tracking, consequential choices, relationship changes, and the first lead toward the concealed underworld. Social interaction remains essential because NPC conversations reveal and change these situations, but housing routines, work, shopping, and long-term life management do not lead the first impression.

The player should feel that:

- Each important NPC is a distinct person rather than a generic chatbot.
- NPCs remember major interactions with the protagonist.
- Different NPCs know different things.
- Information does not automatically spread to every character.
- Discovering a genuine common interest has a persistent effect.
- An NPC's Familiarity, Trust, and Attraction can change independently over time.
- NPCs can express emotion, continue or end conversations, and propose permitted actions.
- The protagonist's relationships differ across save slots based on what happened in that playthrough.

### 3.4 Primary repeating loop

The locked primary loop is:

```text
Talk to people
  → discover a lead, problem, opportunity, or secret
  → decide whether and how to pursue it
  → complete, alter, betray, or abandon a risky quest
  → apply believable consequences to relationships, factions, and world state
  → unlock new conversations, opportunities, and leads
  → repeat
```

Conversation is the main way the player discovers and changes situations, but dialogue does not automatically complete authoritative objectives. Deterministic game systems track quest requirements, player actions, items, locations, time, danger, faction standing, and results. Qwen may propose reactions and interpretations after the outcome is known.

Life-management systems such as money, schedules, reputation, and the weekly allowance create pressure and choices inside this loop. They support the social adventure rather than forming an equally weighted second game.

Consequences use two connected layers:

- **Social consequences** are the normal result of risky choices. They include trust changes, rejection, betrayal, damaged relationships, reputation changes, closed opportunities, and changed faction access.
- **Practical consequences** are reserved for more serious actions. They may include lost money or items, police attention, arrest, injury, or death.

The severity must be understandable from the player's action, the involved characters, and established island rules. A cute presentation or dark joke does not erase the simulated consequence.

Violence is resolved through contextual actions rather than twitch combat. A player selects a permitted action against a nearby target or situation; deterministic code evaluates position, equipment, health, prior preparation, witnesses, and relevant quest state. A short abstract animation presents the result.

Criminal actions create evidence and may be witnessed. Police attention progresses through `noticed`, `questioned`, `wanted`, and `arrest-on-sight`. Defeat does not permanently kill the protagonist. It may cause injury, arrest, lost time, lost money or items, and reputation damage. NPC death is permitted when an authored or validated outcome supports it.

### 3.5 Time flow

The world advances continuously during ordinary exploration. The player can pause the simulation or run it at `1×` or `2×` speed. At `1×`, one real second advances the authoritative clock by one game minute, so a complete twenty-four-hour day takes `24` real minutes. At `2×`, a complete day takes `12` real minutes. Movement, NPC schedules, quest timers, weather, and other deterministic simulation systems use that clock.

Starting an NPC conversation automatically pauses that clock. The world stays paused while the player reads, writes, or waits for a locally generated response. The clock resumes at the player's selected speed when the conversation ends. Menus may pause time when their specific design requires it; those menu rules remain to be defined.

The villa bed provides two actions:

- **Nap:** available at any time; advances the clock and off-screen schedules by two game hours and restores some energy.
- **Sleep until morning:** available after `8:00 PM`; advances the clock and off-screen schedules to `8:00 AM`, restores more energy, and skips the remaining night.

Energy and Health each use a visible `0–100` scale. A two-hour nap restores approximately `25` Energy and overnight sleep restores approximately `80` Energy, subject to focused balance testing. The game does not force the protagonist to return home by a curfew.

### 3.6 Allowance and quest economy

The protagonist receives a regular weekly allowance as part of his prize. It is a safety net that pays for seven days of ordinary food and basic daily costs plus a small buffer. The player does not need a normal job to preserve a basic life, and routine survival pressure does not replace the social-adventure loop.

Risky quests and optional opportunities provide additional money. An ordinary quest may pay up to one weekly allowance; a dangerous quest may pay one to three weekly allowances. That discretionary money pays for gifts, entertainment, bribes, equipment, luxuries, and other advantages that can open or change social and faction routes. Money remains authoritative deterministic state; dialogue may discuss or propose a transaction, but game code validates and applies every payment and reward.

### 3.7 Light personal needs

The protagonist has two personal-condition values:

- **Energy (`0–100`)** represents tiredness and readiness for demanding activity. It drains slowly while the protagonist is awake and from defined demanding actions. Food and sleep can restore it. Low Energy may reduce or block strenuous choices, but it does not create a separate starvation or survival game.
- **Health (`0–100`)** represents injury and physical condition. It does not drain passively. Serious danger, violence, drugs, or other defined events may reduce it. Food, sleep, time, treatment, or items may support recovery according to deterministic rules.

There are no separate hunger, hygiene, bladder, fun, or social-need meters. Eating and sleeping are useful supporting actions rather than chores that constantly interrupt the primary loop. Relationships track social outcomes; they are not represented by a generic social-need bar.

### 3.8 Starting villa

The prize includes a small private villa in the northwest residential and relaxation neighborhood. The villa is the protagonist's starting location and contains:

- A bedroom for sleep and energy recovery
- A bathroom as part of the believable home layout, without a hygiene or bladder meter
- A kitchen for food use and basic preparation interactions
- Storage for items and equipment
- A social area where invited characters can visit

The villa is a normal same-map building with a pathfindable door and roof occlusion. It provides a useful home base without turning home maintenance into the primary loop.

Home invitations begin as validated dialogue actions. An invited NPC may accept, reject, or suggest another time according to schedule, relationship state, boundaries, and current circumstances. An accepted invitation becomes a deterministic scheduled event; the NPC travels normally rather than teleporting to the villa.

### 3.9 Lead and quest journal

The player has a journal that records validated information discovered during play:

- Leads, rumors, problems, and opportunities
- Confirmed clues
- Deadlines the protagonist has actually learned
- General location descriptions
- Exact known locations
- Current resolution state and important outcomes

A journal entry does not receive a map marker merely because it exists. The marker appears only after conversation, observation, an item, or another deterministic event validates an exact location. A vague clue such as "near the beach" remains text until the protagonist learns the specific building or map position. Qwen may propose a journal candidate, but deterministic game code validates its source, quest identifier, deadline, location precision, and state before updating the journal.

## 4. Application Stack and Local LLM Runtime

### 4.0 Application stack

SI World copies the established Life Sim software stack:

| Layer | Choice |
|---|---|
| Language | Strict TypeScript |
| Application and UI | Expo + React Native + React Native Web |
| World renderer | `@shopify/react-native-skia`, using atlas-based sprite rendering |
| Animation | `react-native-reanimated` shared values and worklets |
| UI state | Zustand; deterministic simulation state remains authoritative |
| Content validation | Zod schemas for authored structured data |
| Styling | React Native `StyleSheet` |
| Audio | `expo-audio` |
| Persistence boundary | Adapter-based persistence with versioned saves; the final desktop storage driver remains open |

This decision copies the technical foundation, not Life Sim's game design. SI World retains direct world exploration, AI NPC conversations, its own art direction, and its own simulation rules.

Life Sim exports its React Native Web application for browser hosting. SI World uses **Electron** as its macOS and Windows desktop shell. The Electron main process owns native desktop integration, versioned save-file access, application lifecycle, and supervision of the bundled model process. The Expo web build runs in the Electron renderer.

### 4.0.1 World presentation

The world is a top-down orthographic 2D tile map divided into four `64×48`-tile neighborhoods. The neighborhoods form a connected `2×2` grid:

```text
Residential and relaxation ↔ Downtown entertainment
            ↕                         ↕
      Commercial          ↔    Docks and government
```

The camera can move independently inside the current neighborhood. Holding the middle mouse button and dragging pans the camera. Optional edge panning may be enabled in settings. The mouse wheel cycles through discrete `1×`, `2×`, and `3×` zoom levels, with `1×` providing the broad neighborhood view and higher levels providing closer character views. Pressing `F` centers the camera on the protagonist. RimWorld and Prison Architect are control-and-map references only; their art, building systems, indirect pawn control, and game rules are not automatically inherited.

The protagonist is controlled through mouse clicks rather than required WASD movement. A click on a reachable destination creates a deterministic pathfinding request; movement, collision, arrival, and interruption remain conventional game-code responsibilities rather than LLM decisions.

Walkable building interiors share the neighborhood tile map. Roofs are a separate occlusion layer grouped by building. A roof is visible while the protagonist is outside its building, then hides or fades when the protagonist crosses through a valid entrance into that building. Exterior walls remain visible so the room shape and exits stay readable. The roof returns after the protagonist leaves. Doors and indoor destinations use the same click-to-move and pathfinding system as outdoor tiles; ordinary buildings do not load separate interior scenes.

The logical map tile is `32×32` pixels. An ordinary character occupies a `24×30` sprite cell positioned inside one tile, matching Hero Football Manager's existing match-character scale while leaving room for a contact shadow and readable separation from surrounding objects. On-screen zoom uses nearest-neighbor scaling at the locked whole-number prototype levels so hard pixel edges remain intact. The prototype does not scale the world below native `1×`.

SI World reuses Hero Football Manager's heroic-chibi character grammar and generation architecture: exaggerated readable faces, varied hair and silhouettes, hard-pixel palette ramps, and separate expressive conversation portraits. It does not copy football kits. SI World characters receive original clothing and the directional movement poses required by this game. Character source art is divided into legs, torso/clothing, head/face, hair, accessory, and an optional held item. These layers are combined during asset generation into flat cells in a runtime sprite atlas.

Each ordinary character's runtime atlas contains eight final walking cells: `front-1`, `front-2`, `rear-1`, `rear-2`, `left-1`, `left-2`, `right-1`, and `right-2`. Artists author two front walking frames and two reusable lateral leg shapes. The generator derives the two rear frames using HFM's existing rear-view method. It produces the left and right cells by keeping the readable front-facing head and torso and combining them with the lateral leg shapes. Ordinary walking alternates its two cells every `130–160 ms`.

World characters use an upright billboard presentation over the top-down environment. Predominantly upward movement selects the rear pair. Downward movement selects the front pair. Left and right movement selects the corresponding generated lateral pair. A one-pixel body lean, a one-pixel contact-shadow shift, and a small vertical bounce can reinforce movement without new art. Talking can use a one-pixel head or body bob.

Full left/right profile sprites are intentionally excluded from the initial system. The prototype must test horizontal movement at the native `1×` neighborhood view before the art bill expands. If the front-facing horizontal walk is unclear, the first fallback is a mirrored three-quarter head/hair view over the existing front torso and lateral legs. A complete side-facing body is considered only if that smaller change also fails.

### 4.1 Foundation model

- **Target model:** Qwen3.5-9B
- **Licence:** Apache License 2.0
- **Commercial use:** Permitted subject to the model's licence, attribution, notice, and redistribution requirements
- **Deployment format:** Quantized GGUF
- **Target quantization:** `Q4_K_M`
- **Runtime:** Bundled `llama-server` from `llama.cpp`

Qwen3.5-9B at `Q4_K_M` is a performance-gated target, not an unconditional ship decision. The initial minimum-memory target is a machine with `16 GB` of system RAM. On the named baseline hardware, conversation generation must reach time to first visible text of no more than `3` seconds, sustained generation of at least `8` tokens per second, and a stable `60 FPS` world renderer while generation is active. If the 9B target fails any gate after focused runtime tuning, the game ships a smaller pinned bundled model that passes all gates. The exact GGUF artifacts and hashes remain release decisions.

### 4.2 Model lifecycle

```text
Launch game
  → Electron starts the bundled llama-server child process on loopback
  → Electron verifies server health and loads the selected local model
  → enter gameplay
  → keep the selected model loaded for the entire gameplay session
  → reuse the same model for every NPC conversation
  → stop the child process when gameplay ends or the game exits
```

There must never be one model instance per NPC. All NPCs share the loaded model and submit conversation requests through a managed inference queue. Electron starts, health-checks, restarts after a verified crash, and cleanly stops the child process. The server binds to a loopback address only. The player does not install or manage Ollama or a separate server.

### 4.3 NPC conversation-context lifecycle

The model remains loaded, but the active NPC context changes:

```text
Player approaches Linda
  → prefetch or parse Linda's context

Player begins talking
  → load/assemble Linda's personality, biography, interaction state,
    permitted world knowledge, current scene, and recent conversation
  → generate responses through the shared selected local model

Player leaves
  → validate proposed changes
  → save approved memories, facts, unlocks, and relationship changes
  → release Linda's active conversation/KV context
  → keep the selected local model loaded
```

Nearby characters may be prefetched, and recently used static character context may be retained in a small cache to reduce response latency.

## 5. Character Content Architecture

### 5.1 Installed authored content

Authored character files ship with the game and are treated as immutable during a playthrough.

```text
content/
  characters/
    linda/
      personality.md
      biography.md
      portrait.png              # future/optional
      voice.json                # future/optional

    sarah/
      personality.md
      biography.md

    main-character/
      personality.md
      biography.md
```

### 5.2 `personality.md`

`personality.md` defines how the character behaves and communicates.

It may include:

- Core temperament
- Decision priorities
- Goals and motivations
- Conversational style
- Speech patterns
- Emotional tendencies
- Likes and dislikes that influence behavior
- Social boundaries
- Romantic boundaries
- Conditions under which the NPC continues or ends a conversation
- Rules against inventing knowledge or granting unauthorized gameplay rewards

Example:

```markdown
---
npc_id: linda
---

# Personality
```

### 5.4 `interaction.md`

`interaction.md` is the generated, save-specific prompt view that contains the major ways the protagonist's interactions have affected the NPC, along with what that NPC knows or believes about him. Versioned JSON remains authoritative.

It must not contain the entire conversation transcript. It should contain compact, important, prompt-relevant information.

Example:

```markdown
---
npc_id: linda
schema_version: 1
relationship:
  familiarity: 4
  trust: 5
  attraction: 2
known_player_facts:
  - fact_id: protagonist-has-cat
    value: true
    learned_during: conversation-0042
common_interests:
  - interest_id: cats
    unlocked: true
    unlocked_during: conversation-0042
---

# Major interaction notes

- Linda is familiar with the main character, has moderate trust in him, and has low attraction toward him.
- Linda knows that the main character owns a cat.
- Linda and the main character discovered a shared interest in cats.
- He previously helped her with a bakery delivery.
```

The structured frontmatter is authoritative for game logic. The prose gives Qwen concise natural-language context.

## 6. Knowledge Projection

An NPC must not receive the whole game save. Each NPC receives a compact projection containing only:

- What the NPC knows about the protagonist
- What the NPC believes about the protagonist
- Major interactions involving that NPC
- The NPC's relationship state with the protagonist
- Relevant memories
- Relevant public world information
- Events the NPC witnessed or was told about
- Facts the NPC can perceive in the current scene

Example:

```text
Game truth:
- The protagonist owns a cat named Pepper.

Linda knows:
- The protagonist owns a cat.

Linda does not yet know:
- The cat's name is Pepper.

Sarah knows:
- Nothing about the protagonist's cat.
```

This separation reduces prompt size and allows information asymmetry, secrets, gossip, misunderstandings, and different relationships across the cast.

NPCs may eventually hold beliefs that are incorrect. Beliefs must be represented separately from authoritative world truth.

## 7. Unlocks and Persistent Conversation Effects

Conversations can propose persistent changes such as:

- Learning a fact about the protagonist
- Discovering a common interest
- Remembering a major event
- Proposing bounded Familiarity, Trust, or Attraction signals for deterministic validation
- Changing trust, familiarity, or attraction
- Unlocking a conversation topic
- Unlocking a location, invitation, activity, quest, or social opportunity
- Causing the NPC to continue or end an interaction

### 7.1 Unique unlock identifiers

A common interest should be stored using a stable unique identifier:

```yaml
common_interests:
  - interest_id: cats
    unlocked: true
```

The displayed common-interest count is derived from unique unlocked interests. Repeating the same fact must not repeatedly award `common_interest +1`.

### 7.2 LLM proposes; game validates

Before each generation, deterministic code builds scene-specific closed registries of permitted fact IDs, interest IDs, action IDs, memory subjects, and unlock IDs. The model receives only candidates that the active NPC could learn, perceive, discuss, or attempt in the current scene. It cannot create a new persistent ID.

The model returns schema-constrained JSON that uses closed enums and IDs. For example:

```json
{
  "dialogue": "Really? What kind of cat do you have?",
  "emotion": "pleasantly_surprised",
  "intent": "continue_conversation",
  "proposed_action": {
    "type": "ask_followup_question",
    "topic": "player_cat"
  },
  "knowledge_candidates": [
    {
      "fact_id": "protagonist-has-cat",
      "value": true,
      "source_message_id": "player-turn-0042",
      "evidence_text": "I have a cat."
    }
  ],
  "unlock_candidates": [
    {
      "type": "common_interest",
      "interest_id": "cats"
    }
  ],
  "memory_candidate": {
    "summary": "Linda learned that the protagonist owns a cat and discovered that they both like cats.",
    "importance": 0.6
  }
}
```

The deterministic game layer checks that:

- Every returned ID was in the registry supplied for this scene.
- The cited source message exists and the evidence text is an exact substring of it.
- The NPC can understand or perceive it.
- The NPC's biography supports the claimed common interest.
- The unlock has not already occurred.
- The proposed state change is permitted and within defined bounds.
- The candidate does not contradict authoritative world state or a permanent authored boundary.

If output fails schema parsing or validation, the game retries generation once with a compact correction message. If the retry also fails, the game displays safe fallback dialogue and applies no persistent proposal. Qwen is never permitted to write directly to the save slot.

### 7.3 Relationship state and romantic permission

Each important NPC stores three separate deterministic values for the protagonist:

- **Familiarity:** how well the NPC knows him and how much shared history exists.
- **Trust:** how safe, reliable, and honest the NPC believes him to be.
- **Attraction:** the NPC's current romantic or sexual interest in him.

These values may rise or fall independently. Familiarity does not guarantee trust. Trust does not guarantee attraction. Attraction does not grant romantic access.

Any romantic action must also pass authored checks for compatibility, personal boundaries, current circumstances, and direct consent. An NPC may reject the protagonist despite high values, and deterministic validation must block any generated action that contradicts an authored hard boundary or lacks consent. Qwen may propose relationship signals and dialogue; only game code applies bounded value changes or stage transitions.

The relationship stages are:

```text
Stranger → Acquaintance → Friend → Dating → Partner → Engaged → Married
```

No stage transition occurs from numbers alone. The required Familiarity, Trust, and Attraction values must be met, and the NPC's authored compatibility, boundaries, current circumstances, and direct consent must permit the transition.

Rejection stores a reason. A reason tied to a changeable circumstance may be reconsidered only after that circumstance genuinely changes. An authored hard boundary is permanent and cannot be worn down through repeated attempts, gifts, high values, or generated dialogue.

## 8. Qwen Responsibilities

Qwen handles:

- What the NPC says
- The NPC's current conversational intent
- Which emotion the NPC expresses
- Which permitted action the NPC would like to attempt
- Whether the NPC wants to continue or leave
- Which event may be memorable
- Candidate facts the NPC learned
- Candidate common-interest discoveries
- Candidate relationship signals
- Concise memory summaries

All structured output uses the current scene's closed schema and candidate registries. Free-text dialogue cannot itself create a quest, reward, relationship transition, invitation, fact, memory, or unlock.

## 9. Deterministic Game Responsibilities

Conventional game code remains authoritative over:

- Player movement
- NPC movement and pathfinding
- Daily schedules
- Collision and animation
- Time and weather
- Inventory
- Money and items
- Quests and progression
- Journal leads, clues, deadlines, and resolution state
- Whether the protagonist has learned an exact location that permits a map marker
- Relationship numbers
- Unlock uniqueness
- Locations and permissions
- Save/load behavior
- Which world facts each NPC may receive
- Validation of every LLM-proposed action or persistent change
- Construction of every scene-specific permitted-candidate registry
- Scheduling, acceptance, rejection, or counter-proposal of invitations

Generated dialogue is never the authoritative source for inventory, quests, progression, or world truth.

## 10. World Architecture

### 10.1 Authored world content

```text
content/
  world/
    setting.md
    history.md
    social-rules.md
    locations/
    factions/
    characters/
```

This directory contains invariant setting and lore authored with the game.

### 10.2 Dynamic world state

Each save slot stores changing world information in authoritative `state.json`. It can generate these prompt views when needed:

```text
save-slot/
  state.json
  prompt-views/
    world.md
```

Dynamic world information may include:

- Current day and time
- Weather
- Active events
- Completed events
- Unlocked locations
- Public knowledge
- Character schedules
- Current location conditions

Only relevant world information is supplied to the active NPC.

### 10.3 Neighborhood maps and transitions

Each of the four neighborhoods is stored as a separate `64×48` tile map. Cardinal boundary connections follow the locked `2×2` layout; diagonal travel does not skip across a corner.

- **Northwest — residential and relaxation:** villas, ordinary homes, spas, massage businesses, relaxation venues, and a beach.
- **Northeast — downtown entertainment:** bars, clubs, nightlife, entertainment, and visible or concealed vice.
- **Southwest — commercial:** restaurants, shopping malls, independent shops, and other retail businesses.
- **Southeast — docks and government:** the docks, port activity, police station, and the governing body of the island.

Exact district names, streets, building counts, and landmark layouts remain open. The functional identities and their positions in the `2×2` grid are locked.

The southeast docks include a visible ferry or ferry terminal to make the island's connection to the outside world believable. Version one does not let the player board it, buy a ticket, or leave through it. The fiction still treats the protagonist as legally free to leave; playable departure is outside version one's scope.

When the protagonist reaches a connected boundary exit, the game:

```text
Pause local movement
  → fade out briefly
  → unload the active neighborhood renderer and local pathfinding scene
  → load the adjacent neighborhood
  → place the protagonist at the matching opposite-edge entrance
  → fade in
  → resume at the previously selected simulation speed
```

Only the active neighborhood is rendered and receives frame-by-frame movement simulation. Inactive neighborhoods retain authoritative state and advance NPCs through lightweight deterministic schedule milestones and event updates. They do not render characters or run continuous pathfinding while inactive.

The protagonist starts in the northwest residential and relaxation neighborhood. The first prototype fully authors that neighborhood. The downtown entertainment, commercial, and docks/government neighborhoods may use simple placeholder ground, boundaries, and entrances, but all valid crossings and return crossings must work. This proves the four-map architecture without requiring four complete content areas immediately.

### 10.4 Buildings and roof occlusion

Building interiors are authored directly inside their neighborhood map. Each walkable building defines:

- Its exterior walls and valid door tiles
- Its interior floor and collision tiles
- A roof-occlusion group
- Indoor props and interaction points
- The tiles that count as being inside the building

When the protagonist enters through a valid door, the building's roof group hides or fades while its walls, floor, furniture, characters, and interaction points remain visible. When the protagonist leaves, the roof returns. NPCs use the same interior pathfinding and doors. The first prototype may use one roof group per building; later room-by-room roof groups remain optional.

The first prototype's required walkable interior is the protagonist's private villa. Its bedroom, bathroom, kitchen, storage, and social area may be compact, but each area must be visually readable and its required interaction point must be reachable through normal pathfinding.

## 12. Save-Slot Architecture

Mutable files must be stored on the player's computer in the platform-appropriate application-data/save location, not under the Steam installation directory.

The player may manually save from any stable gameplay state. Manual saving is unavailable while an NPC conversation is active or while a neighborhood transition is in progress, because those states have not reached a complete deterministic snapshot boundary.

The game also maintains three rotating autosaves. It creates the next autosave after:

- Overnight sleep completes
- A neighborhood transition completes
- A major quest outcome and all validated consequences finish applying

Saving snapshots authoritative deterministic state only. Qwen never writes the save and an unfinished generated response is never treated as committed state.

The authoritative save is versioned JSON. A logical layout is:

```text
save-slots/
  slot-001/
    manifest.json
    state.json
    prompt-views/
      characters/
        linda-interaction.md
        sarah-interaction.md
      main-character.md
      world.md
```

`manifest.json` records the save schema and pinned model, prompt, content, and engine versions. `state.json` is the authoritative deterministic state. Markdown prompt views are generated from validated JSON when context is assembled. They are disposable and cannot override JSON state. The logical `interaction.md` design remains the human-readable model-context view.

Required save-system properties:

- Per-slot isolation
- Atomic or recoverable writes
- Schema versioning
- Duplicate-unlock prevention
- Save migration support
- Corruption handling
- Steam Cloud compatibility
- No direct writes from Qwen
- Manual-save eligibility based on a stable snapshot boundary
- Rotating autosaves after the locked sleep, travel, and quest-outcome triggers
- Exactly three autosave generations retained per save lineage
- Pinned model, prompt, content, and engine versions
- Explicit compatible migrations with no silent change to established NPC behavior

## 13. Large-Cast Runtime Rules

- Version one uses two NPC tiers:
  - **Full-AI named NPCs:** `8–12` important characters with dedicated authored context, free-text Qwen conversations, and persistent interaction state.
  - **Ambient residents:** `20–40` supporting residents with deterministic schedules, pathfinding, authored short dialogue, and no local-model conversation or persistent conversational memory.
- Important full-AI NPCs receive full AI conversations and persistent interaction state.
- Qwen runs only for active conversations or explicitly scheduled high-level decisions.
- Routine walking, schedules, and background simulation do not require continuous LLM inference.
- Each resident has four to six authored daily schedule blocks, such as home, work, meal, leisure, nightlife, and sleep. The exact block set may differ by character and day type.
- Active-neighborhood residents pathfind continuously toward their current schedule target. Inactive-neighborhood residents advance only at deterministic departure, arrival, and event milestones; they do not run hidden frame-by-frame movement.
- The shared local model processes NPC requests through a queue.
- Static character content can be parsed and cached.
- Nearby likely conversation targets can be prefetched.
- Old conversation history is summarized; the whole transcript is not resent indefinitely.
- Persistent memories are limited to major, relevant events.
- Ambient residents may still react through deterministic authored state and schedule changes, but they do not silently become full-AI characters during a playthrough.

## 14. Security, Integrity, and Content Guardrails

- Player dialogue is untrusted input.
- Player attempts to override character instructions must not bypass game rules.
- NPCs cannot grant unauthorized rewards or progression.
- The LLM cannot directly access or modify save files.
- All structured model output is schema-constrained, parsed, and validated against closed scene-specific registries.
- Invalid output receives one correction retry. A second failure produces safe authored fallback dialogue and no persistent change.
- Generated dialogue may discuss fictional adult relationships, consensual adult prostitution, crime, drugs, addiction, and violence when relevant to the character and scene.
- Explicit sexual detail, sexual violence, sexual content involving minors, and sexual content involving real people are not generated. The game refuses the request in character when practical or uses an authored fade to black.
- All romance and sexual context involves fictional adults. Consent and authored boundaries cannot be bypassed by a prompt, relationship score, gift, quest, or generated dialogue.
- The bundled local server binds only to loopback and is not exposed to the network.
- Model and runtime licenses must be distributed with required notices.
- Community fine-tunes or third-party GGUFs require separate provenance and licence review.
- Runtime-generated NPC dialogue must be disclosed to Steam as live-generated AI content.
- SI World must document guardrails against illegal or otherwise prohibited generated content for Steam's AI Content Survey.

## 15. Performance and UX Requirements

- The selected performance-gated local model loads once when gameplay begins.
- The model remains loaded until gameplay ends.
- NPC-specific context is loaded or assembled on demand.
- NPC responses stream as they are generated.
- Ordinary dialogue uses non-thinking/direct-response mode unless a future mechanic explicitly needs deeper reasoning.
- NPC responses should generally be concise and conversational.
- The game provides immediate feedback while generation begins, such as eye contact, a thinking animation, or a conversational pause.
- LLM activity must not block the render/gameplay loop.
- The initial hardware target is a machine with `16 GB` of system RAM.
- On the named baseline hardware, time to first visible generated text must be no more than `3` seconds, sustained generation must be at least `8` tokens per second, and the world renderer must hold `60 FPS` during generation.
- The exact baseline CPU and GPU still require selection and representative testing before final minimum specifications are published.
- If Qwen3.5-9B at `Q4_K_M` fails a gate after focused runtime tuning, a smaller pinned bundled model must replace it.
- macOS and Windows builds require separately verified runtime backends and packaging.
- Dialogue is text-first. Short authored laughs, sighs, greetings, and similar vocal sounds may support it; version one does not generate full spoken dialogue.
- Version one is mouse-and-keyboard desktop software. It does not promise Steam Deck support, but controls and UI architecture must not prevent a later controller and Steam Deck pass.
